    Why measure the energy of learning?

    Training a model uses electricity, and producing electricity emits greenhouse gases. Green AI asks researchers to report efficiency (energy, time, emissions) next to accuracy, so that methods are compared on both [16]. Studies of large models showed how big this footprint can be and why it should be reported in a standard way [17] [18] [19].

    This platform applies that idea to reinforcement learning on route problems. It builds on the author's work on the energy efficiency of machine-learning models [1], on Green Machine Learning practices [2], on raising awareness of energy use [3] and on energy-efficient RL for combinatorial optimisation [4] [5].

    Green Machine Learning looks for models that are accurate and use little energy, at the same time [1].

    Reinforcement learning in one page

    In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent learns by trial and error. At each step it observes a state s, takes an action a, receives a reward r and reaches a new state s′ [9]. It tries to maximise the return, the discounted sum of future rewards:

    Gt = rt+1 + γ rt+2 + γ2 rt+3 + …

    Tabular methods keep a Q-table: one value Q(s, a) per state–action pair, estimating how good it is to take a in s. An episode is one run from the start to the end; here, one complete tour.

    Q-learning [10]

    Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

    Learns from the best next action, whatever it actually does next (off-policy).

    SARSA [11] [9]

    Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

    Learns from the action a′ it really takes next (on-policy). The name lists the five terms: s, a, r, s′, a′.

    Double Q-learning [12]

    QA(s,a) ← QA(s,a) + α [ r + γ QB(s′, argmaxa′ QA(s′,a′)) − QA(s,a) ]

    Two tables: one picks the next action, the other values it. This reduces the overestimation caused by the max in Q-learning.

    The settings you choose

    Learning rate α
    How far each update moves Q towards the new estimate. Near 0 the agent learns slowly; near 1 it follows its latest experience.
    Discount factor γ
    How much future rewards count. Near 0 the agent is short-sighted; near 1 it values the whole rest of the tour.
    Exploration ε (ε-greedy)
    With probability ε the agent tries a random action; otherwise it takes the best one in its Q-table [9]. ε₀ is the value at the start.
    Epsilon decay
    How ε shrinks over the episodes: linear (constant rate), convex (fast at first, then slower), concave (slow at first, then faster), step (in jumps) or fixed (no change). unknown means the data does not record it.
    Reward R1–R3
    Three reward functions compared in the experiments. Each turns the cost of a move into a reinforcement; a classic choice is rij = −dij, so longer moves are penalised more [7]. Several such functions are compared in [8].
    Seed
    The random seed of one repetition. Same settings with another seed give another run of the same experiment: compare seeds to see how much results vary.

    The grid of α and γ values (0.01 to 0.99) follows the parameter study of Ottoni et al. [7].

    The travelling salesman problem as RL

    The travelling salesman problem (TSP) asks for the shortest tour that visits every city once and returns to the start. In the asymmetric TSP (ATSP) going from i to j may cost more or less than going back.

    Statethe city where the agent is
    Actionthe next city, among those not yet visited
    Rewarda penalty from the distance travelled, e.g. −dij [7]
    Episodeone complete tour
    Distancethe length of that tour; lower is better

    The instances come from TSPLIB, a public library of benchmark problems with known optimal tour lengths [13]. The gap to optimum says how far the best tour is from that optimum:

    gap = (best − optimum) ÷ optimum × 100%

    RL for routing goes back to Ant-Q [14]; a survey of RL for combinatorial optimisation is [15]. Related work by the author's group covers parameter tuning [7] [8] and transfer learning between problems [6].

    How energy is measured

    Energy is given in kilowatt-hours (kWh): 1 kWh is 1,000 W used for one hour. The values here are tiny, around 10−9 kWh per episode, because one tabular episode lasts less than a millisecond.

    CPU
    Read from RAPL (Running Average Power Limit), energy counters built into Intel and AMD processors [20] [21].
    RAM
    Not measured directly: CodeCarbon estimates it from a fixed power per memory module multiplied by time [20]. That is why the RAM line is almost flat.
    Energy E
    CPU + RAM (+ GPU, not used in these runs).
    Duration
    Wall-clock time of the episode.
    Tracking mode
    machine measures the whole computer; process estimates only the share of the training process [20]. The runs in this dataset used machine mode, with CPU energy from RAPL.

    From energy to emissions

    CO₂-equivalent (CO₂eq) expresses different greenhouse gases as the amount of CO₂ with the same warming effect [22]. The carbon intensity C of a grid is how much CO₂eq it emits per kWh. It depends on the energy mix: coal and gas raise it; hydro, wind, solar and nuclear lower it [20].

    CO₂eq = E × C   (E in kWh, C in g CO₂eq/kWh)

    The country values come from CodeCarbon's energy-mix data, which use Our World in Data [20] [23]. Only C changes between countries, so the same run can emit tens of times more in one country than in another: see the map in Explore a run.

    Everyday comparisons

    Very small numbers are hard to picture, so the page divides them by a familiar amount: equivalent = amount ÷ factor. The factors come from the US EPA [24], a Google study of AI prompts [25] and the EcoLogits calculator [26]. If you ran this every day multiplies the amount by 30 and 365.

    • They show orders of magnitude, not exact equivalence.
    • Walking is the body's energy (from food), not electricity.
    • The home figure is a US average.

    Reading the charts

    Per episode
    Thousands of episodes are grouped into steps: the line is each step's mean, the band its minimum and maximum.
    Cumulative
    The running total from the first episode.
    Best so far
    The shortest tour found up to each episode; it can only go down. marks an episode that sets a new best.
    Compare
    Two runs side by side; ✓ marks the lower (better) value.
