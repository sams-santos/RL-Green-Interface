Welcome to ECOVIS!

ECOVIS is here to help you see the good in green and energy-efficient reinforcement learning. It is an interactive energy analysis platform that shows how much energy tabular RL agents (Q-learning, SARSA and Double Q-learning) use while they learn to solve travelling salesman problems. Pick an experiment, replay its 1,000 episodes, see the energy consumption, emissions and tour quality of every episode, and gain insights into Sustainable Computing.

ECOVIS was born out of a simple yet powerful idea: to raise awareness about the energy consumption of machine learning. In a world where data-driven decisions are becoming the norm, the environmental impact of these processes often goes unnoticed. We wanted to change that.

Here that idea meets reinforcement learning. Each of the 69,120 runs was measured while it trained, so you can see how the algorithm, the reward, the exploration schedule and the α and γ settings change both the tour the agent finds and the energy it takes to find it. The platform is designed to be simple, intuitive and insightful, helping you understand the results and also think about the energy behind them.

The first version of ECOVIS, built for machine-learning models, was created by Laís C. Medeiros and Samara Santos, who designed its name, logo and look. This version brings it to reinforcement learning.

We believe that small changes in the way we approach machine learning can have a big impact on our planet. Join us in making machine learning greener!

Why measure the energy of learning?

Training a model uses electricity, and producing electricity emits greenhouse gases. Green AI asks researchers to report efficiency (energy, time, emissions) next to accuracy, so that methods are compared on both [16]. Studies of large models showed how big this footprint can be and why it should be reported in a standard way [17] [18] [19].

This platform applies that idea to reinforcement learning on route problems. It builds on the author's work on the energy efficiency of machine-learning models [1], on Green Machine Learning practices [2], on raising awareness of energy use [3] and on energy-efficient RL for combinatorial optimisation [4] [5].

Green Machine Learning looks for models that are accurate and use little energy, at the same time [1].

Reinforcement learning in one page

In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent learns by trial and error. At each step it observes a state s, takes an action a, receives a reward r and reaches a new state s′ [9]. It tries to maximise the return, the discounted sum of future rewards:

Gt = rt+1 + γ rt+2 + γ2 rt+3 + …

Tabular methods keep a Q-table: one value Q(s, a) per state–action pair, estimating how good it is to take a in s. An episode is one run from the start to the end; here, one complete tour.

Q-learning [10]

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

Learns from the best next action, whatever it actually does next (off-policy).

SARSA [11] [9]

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

Learns from the action a′ it really takes next (on-policy). The name lists the five terms: s, a, r, s′, a′.

Double Q-learning [12]

QA(s,a) ← QA(s,a) + α [ r + γ QB(s′, argmaxa′ QA(s′,a′)) − QA(s,a) ]

Two tables: one picks the next action, the other values it. This reduces the overestimation caused by the max in Q-learning.

The settings you choose

Learning rate α
How far each update moves Q towards the new estimate. Near 0 the agent learns slowly; near 1 it follows its latest experience.
Discount factor γ
How much future rewards count. Near 0 the agent is short-sighted; near 1 it values the whole rest of the tour.
Exploration ε (ε-greedy)
With probability ε the agent tries a random action; otherwise it takes the best one in its Q-table [9]. ε₀ is the value at the start.
Epsilon decay
How ε changes over the 1,000 episodes in this dataset: linear falls at a constant rate to ε₀/1000; concave falls faster at first, then more slowly, ending near 0.37 ε₀; convex stays near ε₀ and then drops steeply towards the end; fixed never changes. step is ε₀ (1 − 0.1 ⌊t/1000⌋): it would fall by 10% every 1,000 episodes, so within these 1,000 episodes it never falls and its runs behave like fixed (same best distances) [29].
Reward R1–R3
How the length dij of a move becomes a reinforcement [29]: R1: r = 1/dij  ·  R2: r = −dij  ·  R3: r = −dij2 R2 is the classic choice of [7]; R3 penalises long moves more strongly. R1 is the only positive reward: since the Q-table starts at zero, moves already tried look better than untried ones, so the agent explores less. Negative rewards do the opposite, which works like optimistic initial values [9]. In the data, R1 runs end with much larger gaps.
Seed
The random seed of a run. Every run in this dataset uses seed 42, so each configuration was trained once.

The grid of α and γ values (0.01 to 0.99) follows the parameter study of Ottoni et al. [7]. The dataset covers every combination: 3 algorithms × 8 instances × 3 rewards × 5 decays × 3 ε₀ × 8 γ × 8 α = 69,120 runs of 1,000 episodes each.

The travelling salesman problem as RL

The travelling salesman problem (TSP) asks for the shortest tour that visits every city once and returns to the start. In the asymmetric TSP (ATSP) going from i to j may cost more or less than going back.

Statethe city where the agent is
Actionthe next city, among those not yet visited
Rewardfrom the move length: 1/dij, −dij or −dij2 (R1–R3) [29]
Episodeone complete tour, from a random start city; the Q-table starts at zero and is kept between episodes [29]
Distancethe length of that tour; lower is better

The instances come from TSPLIB, a public library of benchmark problems with known optimal tour lengths [13]. The gap to optimum says how far the best tour is from that optimum:

gap = (best − optimum) ÷ optimum × 100%

RL for routing goes back to Ant-Q [14]; a survey of RL for combinatorial optimisation is [15]. Related work by the author's group covers parameter tuning [7] [8] and transfer learning between problems [6].

How energy is measured

Energy is given in kilowatt-hours (kWh): 1 kWh is 1,000 W used for one hour. The values here are tiny, around 10−9 kWh per episode, because one tabular episode lasts less than a millisecond.

CPU
Read from RAPL (Running Average Power Limit), energy counters built into Intel and AMD processors [20] [21]. CodeCarbon 3.3.1 wraps each episode in start_task / stop_task: the episode's energy is the difference between the counter readings at the start and at the end [29]. The tracker was set to add the DRAM domain to this reading and to prefer the platform (psys) domain when the machine has one, so the CPU column can cover more than the processor [20].
Zero CPU episodes
An episode lasts about one millisecond. If the RAPL counter has not changed between the two readings, the difference is 0 and that energy appears in a later episode; Khan et al. discuss these timing limits of RAPL readings [21]. About a quarter of all episodes record 0 CPU energy (up to two thirds on the smallest instance). Totals over many episodes are reliable; a single episode is not.
RAM
Not measured directly: CodeCarbon estimates it from a fixed power per memory module multiplied by time (20 W in these runs) [20]. That is why the RAM line is almost flat. CodeCarbon leaves the DRAM domain out of the CPU reading by default so that memory is not counted twice; these runs included it, so part of the memory energy may appear in both columns [20].
Energy E
CPU + RAM (+ GPU, not used in these runs).
Duration
Wall-clock time of the episode.
Tracking mode
machine measures the whole computer; process estimates only the share of the training process [20]. The runs in this dataset used machine mode, with CPU energy from RAPL.

From energy to emissions

CO₂-equivalent (CO₂eq) expresses different greenhouse gases as the amount of CO₂ with the same warming effect [22]. The carbon intensity C of a grid is how much CO₂eq it emits per kWh. It depends on the energy mix: coal and gas raise it; hydro, wind, solar and nuclear lower it [20].

CO₂eq = E × C   (E in kWh, C in g CO₂eq/kWh)

The country values come from CodeCarbon's energy-mix data, which use Our World in Data [20] [23]. Only C changes between countries, so the same run can emit tens of times more in one country than in another: see the map in Explore a run.

Everyday comparisons

Very small numbers are hard to picture, so the page divides them by a familiar amount: equivalent = amount ÷ factor. The factors come from the US EPA [24], a Google study of AI prompts [25] and the EcoLogits calculator [26]. If you ran this every day multiplies the amount by 30 and 365.

  • They show orders of magnitude, not exact equivalence.

How the ECOVIS score is calculated

The ECOVIS score follows the method of Fischer et al. for assessing and labelling the efficiency of machine learning [27], in the refined form of their STREP framework [28]. Runs are compared only with runs that solve the same task on the same data in the same environment: here, every run on the same instance (8,640 runs: 3 algorithms × 2,880 settings), all measured on the same machine.

1 · An index for each property

Each property is divided by the best value observed on the instance, so it becomes a number in (0, 1], where 1 is the best result [28, Eq. 1]. Both properties used here should be minimised:

ptour = shortest best tour on the instance ÷ this run's best tour

penergy = lowest total energy on the instance ÷ this run's total energy

An index of 0.5 means “twice the best value”: it keeps how far apart runs are, not just their order [27].

2 · A compound score

The two indices are combined with weights that add up to 1 [28, Eq. 2]. Like STREP, ECOVIS gives the quality group (tour) and the resource group (energy) the same weight by default; the slider What matters more? changes it.

P = (1 − w) × ptour + w × penergy    (w = 0.5 by default)    ECOVIS score = 100 × P

3 · Ratings A–E

Each index, and the score, is split into five ratings at the 20 %, 40 %, 60 % and 80 % quantiles of all its values on the instance [27] [28]. A is the top fifth, E the bottom fifth. The energy label of a run is the rating of its energy index. The labels take their look from the EU energy label, as in [27].

Aat or above the 80 % quantile
B60–80 %
C40–60 %
D20–40 %
Ebelow the 20 % quantile

Example

Q-learning on berlin52 (R1, linear decay, ε₀ = 0.10, α = 0.15, γ = 0.90) finds a best tour of 20,261 and uses 5.613 × 10−6 kWh. On berlin52 the shortest best tour is 7,866 and the lowest energy is 4.041 × 10−6 kWh:

  • ptour = 7,866 ÷ 20,261 ≈ 0.39 → rating E (the 20 % quantile is 0.53)
  • penergy = 4.041 ÷ 5.613 ≈ 0.72 → rating A (the 80 % quantile is 0.60)
  • P = 0.5 × 0.39 + 0.5 × 0.72 ≈ 0.55 → score 55, rating D (quantiles of P: 0.51, 0.57, 0.64, 0.75)

The ratings depend on the runs being compared: as Fischer et al. note, they change when new results are added [28]. ECOVIS compares runs within one instance only.

Reading the charts

Per episode
Thousands of episodes are grouped into steps: the line is each step's mean, the band its minimum and maximum.
Cumulative
The running total from the first episode.
Best so far
The shortest tour found up to each episode; it can only go down. marks an episode that sets a new best.
ECOVIS score
A 0–100 score of a whole run against every run on its instance, with a rating A–E, following Fischer et al. [27] [28]. See How the ECOVIS score is calculated.
α × γ grid
The 64 runs that share every other setting, one cell each, coloured by gap to optimum or total energy. Lighter is lower; ★ marks the lowest; the outlined cell is the open run.
Compare
Two runs side by side; ✓ marks the lower (better) value.
